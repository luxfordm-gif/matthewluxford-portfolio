# Tone of voice — matthewluxford.co.uk

A guide to how I write. Reference for me and for Claude Code when generating new copy for the site.

---

## The short version

Confident, specific, warm, slightly cheeky. Talks like a person, never like a brochure. Earns trust through facts and personality, not adjectives.

If a line could appear on a LinkedIn influencer post or a 2014 design agency homepage, it's wrong.

---

## Voice principles

### 1. Specific beats general
Numbers, names, real dates, real outcomes. "3.4M users", "500% increase in booking enquiries", "27 markets". Not "drove significant growth" or "scaled the platform".

### 2. Plain English, contractions on
"I'm", "don't", "can't", "we've". Read it back — it should sound like me talking. No "utilise", no "leverage", no "facilitate".

### 3. Warmth without cheese
Positive energy is genuine, never performative. "I genuinely love getting stuck in" works. "I'm passionate about creating delightful experiences" doesn't.

### 4. Light self-awareness
Parenthetical asides, footnote disclaimers, gentle self-deprecation. "(apologies in advance)", "(boring but essential)", "* I didn't force them to write these... trust me". These soften credentials without undercutting them.

### 5. Earn the emphasis
Bold for the actually-important. Italics for accent. If everything's emphasised, nothing is.

### 6. Show, don't decorate
"It's an app built by someone who actually uses it. Imagine that." beats "A user-first experience". Concrete examples and specific details over abstract claims.

## Title positioning

**Primary title across the site:** Senior Web Designer.

Exception: in the role-by-role experience history on the CV, use the actual title held at each company (Lead Product Designer at Yoppie, Design Director at LONO, etc.). Those are facts.

Everywhere else — meta tags, About paragraphs, hero subtitles, intro copy — Senior Web Designer.

No "go-to designer", no "creative director" (not a title I've held), no stacked-title mashups ("Senior Web Designer Design Lead · Go-to designer").

## Mechanics

### Punctuation
- **Em-dash (—)** for asides and rhythmic breaks. Real em-dashes, not hyphens.
- **Parentheses** for the cheeky/self-aware aside: "(apologies in advance)", "(boring but essential)".
- **Period over semicolon** in headlines and short lines.
- **No Oxford comma.** UK style. "UX, UI, brand and design systems" — not "UX, UI, brand, and design systems".
- **Ellipses sparingly.** OK in deliberate trailing-off ("WOAH... YOU REALLY LIKE TO SCROLL"). Not as filler.

### Spelling
UK English throughout: "centred", "behaviour", "organisation", "practising", "favourite". Not "centered" or "behavior".

### Capitalisation
- Section headings: sentence case ("Featured work", "Notable achievement", "Outside of work").
- Job title ("Senior Web Designer"): title case.
- Acronyms ALL CAPS: UX, UI, CMS, IA, NDA.

### Numbers
- Digits for stats and emphasis: "3.4M users", "500%", "18+ years".
- Written-out for small/casual: "two kids", "a few months".
- Default to whichever lands harder visually.

### Italics & bold
- **Bold** for the load-bearing fact or commitment.
- *Italics* for accent or product/title names (e.g. *Lifecake*, *The Storyteller Within*).
- Don't double them up. Don't italicise every heading-end — it creeps in over time and becomes a mannerism.

## Things to avoid — the no-go list

- "Passionate / passion"
- "Thoughtful / thoughtfully" (especially in About copy)
- "Innovate / innovative"
- "Leverage" as a verb
- "Utilise" (use "use")
- "Synergy", "value-add", "stakeholders" as a euphemism for real teams
- "Robust", "scalable", "best-in-class" as empty adjectives
- "Thought leader", "rockstar", "ninja", "wizard", "guru"
- "Crafted" (over-used in designer-world; "built" or "designed" is fine)
- "Journey" (unless literally about cycling)
- "User-centric" (be specific about what that meant in practice)
- Emoji used decoratively — they can appear in deliberate moments, kept rare

## Audience

Three readers, roughly equal proportion:

1. **In-house hiring managers and design directors.** Want to know I'm senior, fast, and won't need babysitting. Lead with the title, the years, and a concrete metric.
2. **Founders and agency leads hiring contracts.** Want to know what I'll ship and how soon. Lead with the work, the speed, and the AI workflow.
3. **Other designers.** Peer respect. Confidence without arrogance. Specific tools and methods named.

All three reward: specificity, plain English, evidence of taste, evidence of speed.

## Examples

### Good (already on the site)

> "It's an app built by someone who actually uses it. Imagine that."

Confident, opinionated, slightly cheeky.

> "Father of two (they're my best work)"

Warmth without sentimentality. Parenthetical does the work.

> "Bella, our cavapoo — probably the laziest in the world, but she makes everyone smile."

Specific (the breed, the trait), affectionate, ends on a note that reflects the brand.

> "Crypto moves fast, and so did we."

Sets the scene in seven words. Earns the right to be brief.

> "Where the craft was sharpened."

Closes a six-role consolidation without padding.

### Bad (the kind we're moving away from)

> "Eighteen years building thoughtful digital products that scale."

Generic. Every senior designer's About page.

> "Drove brand consistency and strategic direction across the business while implementing user-centred design practices."

Three corporate clichés in one sentence: "drove", "strategic direction", "implementing... practices".

> "Built foundational digital design skillset across client work."

Means nothing. Reads as filler.

> "Navigating the pace and complexity was invaluable."

Soft-focus self-praise. Tell the reader what the pace actually was — review cycles in hours, not weeks.

## For Claude Code

When generating new copy for this site:

- Pull rhythms from the **Phrases that belong to this site** bank — reword, don't paste.
- Apply the **Mechanics** section as hard rules: em-dashes, UK spelling, no Oxford comma, sentence case for section headings.
- Run any new copy past the **no-go list** before suggesting it.
- When in doubt: write it as if speaking it out loud, then trim.
- Default to brevity. Anything that earns its place can be longer.
- Lead with the fact, not the feeling. Pay off any opener with specifics.
